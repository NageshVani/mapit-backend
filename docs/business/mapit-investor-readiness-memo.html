§0.1

Why this memo exists now

The original plan — bootstrap for ~6 months on Arun and Nagesh's own funding, prove Bangalore demand, then raise — assumed the trust & safety bar was "email OTP is enough for a beta." Researching OfferUp's TruYou program changed that: a real name and government ID plus phone match is now treated as table stakes for a stranger-to-stranger marketplace with a physical meetup, not a nice-to-have. MapIt's own architecture already reflects that instinct — GPS-exact listings, a grievance log built to IT Rules 2021 timelines, admin moderation — so phone verification is a continuation of a pattern already set, not a new philosophy.

The complication is cost, not conviction: phone OTP means a Twilio (or comparable) SMS bill that scales with signups, which breaks the "$0/month until Session 9" budget discipline the project has held since Session 1. That single new recurring line item is what's pushing the conversation from bootstrap six months, then talk to investors to raise a small round first, so the trust layer isn't the thing that gets cut when money is tight.

§A

What investors will actually look for

For a hyperlocal C2C marketplace specifically — not a generic SaaS checklist. Ranked by what a seed-stage investor in this category weighs first.

A.1 — Liquidity in one city, not total signups

The single metric that matters most for a marketplace is whether a listing in Bangalore actually gets a buyer response and a completed meetup — not how many people downloaded or registered. Investors in this category will ask for a match rate (listings that get at least one genuine "I'm Interested"), a time-to-first-response, and a repeat-seller rate (do people who sold once come back to sell again). MapIt already has the plumbing to produce these numbers (interest notes, chat threads, admin overview dashboard) — the gap isn't tooling, it's real transaction volume from a public Bangalore audience rather than family and friends.

A.2 — Trust & safety as infrastructure, not a feature

This is the direct payoff of the TruYou research. An investor who has seen a marketplace fail on fraud or safety incidents will look specifically for: identity verification stronger than email, a reporting/moderation path (MapIt has this — Session 6 report-listing + Session 8 reported-chat visibility), a grievance/complaint SLA (MapIt has this — the 24-hour/15-day IT Rules grievance log), and evidence the founders treat this as core, not an afterthought. Phone verification closes the one visible gap in an otherwise unusually complete trust stack for an MVP this size.

A.3 — A monetization thesis, even pre-revenue

Nobody expects revenue at this stage, but they will expect a specific answer to "how does this make money once it works" — not "we'll figure it out." Reasonable levers already implied by the product: a per-listing or subscription fee once density is proven, promoted/featured listings, a paid trust badge (phone+ID verified sellers rank higher — this doubles as a trust and monetization feature), and eventually a facilitation fee on higher-value categories like vehicles and real estate where MapIt already has dedicated listing schemas.

A.4 — Team-market fit

Two founders, one technical and shipping fast (nine build sessions, a working chat/moderation/admin stack, largely solo), one with Bangalore ground presence, legal judgment, and investor relations instinct already exercised (the ToS/incorporation research, UAT sign-off discipline). That division of labor is exactly what seed investors look for in a hyperlocal product — someone has to be physically present where the liquidity has to happen.

A.5 — A market size story that survives scrutiny

India's C2C classifieds market is large but crowded (OLX, Quikr, Facebook Marketplace, and a long tail of category apps) and mostly free-to-use, which is exactly why undifferentiated growth pitches get rejected. The defensible story is narrower: exact-GPS, hyperlocal trust as the wedge — starting in one dense, high-income city (Bangalore) where "who am I actually meeting, and how far away are they, really" is a felt problem existing apps answer poorly (locality-only or self-reported location, minimal verification).

A.6 — A cap table and legal foundation they can actually invest into

This is the one that blocks everything else regardless of how good the metrics look: a sole proprietorship cannot issue shares. See §D — this has to be fixed before a term sheet, not after.

What's already diligence-ready

Don't undersell this: ToS/Privacy live and Arun-approved, a real grievance-officer contact under IT Rules 2021, RLS-audited data access, Sentry error monitoring, and an admin ops console most seed-stage MVPs don't have yet. Lead with it — it's evidence of founder discipline, which investors read as a proxy for execution risk.

§B

How much equity to offer

Short answer: don't fix a number yet — fix the instrument, and size the raise to the next real proof point, not to a valuation you can't defend today.

At this stage (single-city, pre-revenue, no phone verification live yet), MapIt is a pre-seed, not a seed, company by Indian market convention — even though the product itself is unusually far along for that stage. Pricing a priced equity round now forces a valuation conversation you don't have the metrics to win. The standard move at this stage in India is a convertible instrument that defers valuation to your next real round:

  • CCPS (Compulsorily Convertible Preference Shares) — the Companies Act–native instrument Indian investors are most comfortable with; converts to equity at the next priced round, usually with a valuation cap and discount.
  • Convertible Note / CCD (Compulsorily Convertible Debenture) — functionally similar to a US SAFE, but SAFEs themselves aren't a recognized instrument under Indian company law, so Indian angel/pre-seed rounds use a CCD dressed the same way (cap + discount, converts later).

Either way, this only becomes possible once MapIt is a Private Limited Company (§D) — a sole proprietorship cannot issue either instrument.

The actual recommendation

Raise only what closes the trust-layer gap and buys 9–12 months of real Bangalore data — not a number that "feels safe." A ₹75L–1Cr pre-seed via CCD/CCPS, priced with a cap rather than a fixed valuation, at roughly 8–10% dilution, funds phone verification (Twilio), a small ops/support hire for Arun in Bangalore, and a public soft-open — without giving away seed-stage equity for pre-seed-stage proof. Reserve an ESOP pool of 10–15% at incorporation (§D) so future hires don't dilute you again later.

§D

Registering MapIt — product and company

Two separate filings with different purposes: a trademark protects the name and brand; company incorporation creates the legal entity that can hold that trademark, sign contracts, and issue equity.

D.1 — Trademark the MapIt name

File with the Indian Trade Marks Registry under Class 35 (marketplace/advertising services) and Class 42 (the software/platform itself) — most consumer apps file both. Run a clearance search first; "MapIt" is short and descriptive-adjacent, so confirm nothing conflicting is already registered before spending on filing. You can use the ™ symbol the moment an application is filed; ® is only correct after registration completes, which typically takes 12–24 months if unopposed. File this under the new company's name (D.2), not Arun's personal name, so the brand asset sits inside the entity investors will actually hold shares in.

D.2 — Incorporate a Private Limited Company

You've already looked at this question once — the earlier LLM-council review (comparing Sole Proprietorship / LLP / Pvt Ltd) concluded a thin sole-proprietorship structure was right for the ToS-liability question specifically. That conclusion doesn't carry over here: fundraising adds a new, harder constraint that overrides it —

Why a sole proprietorship blocks fundraising specifically

A sole proprietorship has no legal identity separate from Arun. It cannot issue shares, cannot have a cap table, and gives Arun unlimited personal liability for anything the business does. No institutional angel or seed fund in India will invest into one. This is not a reversal of the earlier ToS decision — it's a different question (liability shield for users vs. ability to hold equity) getting a different answer.

The standard path: incorporate a Private Limited Company via the MCA's SPICe+ form, registered office in Bangalore, minimum 2 directors and 2 shareholders (Arun and Nagesh both work), each needing a DIN and DSC. The existing sole-proprietorship business — the app, the domain, the trademark once filed, any existing contracts — gets moved into the new company via a Business Transfer Agreement (a "slump sale"), so nothing has to be rebuilt from scratch.
