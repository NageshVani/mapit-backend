Section 1
What is a POI?
POI stands for Point of Interest. It is any named, real-world place that someone might want to know about on a map.
🏫
Education
Schools, colleges, coaching centres, libraries. e.g. "JSS School", "IIM Bangalore"
🏬
Retail & Commerce
Malls, supermarkets, petrol bunks, local kirana stores. e.g. "Forum Mall", "More Supermarket"
🏥
Health & Services
Hospitals, clinics, banks, ATMs, police stations. e.g. "Fortis Hospital", "SBI ATM"
🍽️
Food & Entertainment
Restaurants, cafes, cinemas, parks. e.g. "MTR Restaurant", "PVR Cinemas"
🚌
Transport
Bus stops, metro stations, auto stands, railway stations. e.g. "Koramangala Bus Stand"
⛪
Landmarks
Temples, churches, mosques, parks, government buildings. e.g. "Cubbon Park"
💡
Why does MapIt care about POIs?
When a buyer looks at a listing, the most natural question is: "What is near this property?" Knowing that a flat is 400m from a school and 1.2km from a supermarket is more useful than just coordinates. POIs turn a GPS pin into a neighbourhood story.
Section 2
What is a POI "Label" on a Map?
A label is the name and icon you see printed on top of the map — like "Forum Mall 🛍" or "JSS School 🏫" — at a high enough zoom level.

⚠️
The critical point: labels are drawn from a database the tile server owns
When your browser downloads a map tile from a provider like CartoDB or Google, that image already has the place names drawn on it. The provider drew those names from their own POI database before sending the tile to you. You cannot change which places are labeled — you get whatever is in that provider's database.
🗺️
With rich POI labels (Google Maps)
You see named schools, malls, hospitals, restaurants, auto stands, temples — all labeled on the map at zoom 14+. Every local business with a Google Business Profile appears.
🗺️
Without rich POI labels (CartoDB / OSM)
You see roads, area names (Koramangala, Indiranagar), and major landmarks. Smaller schools, local malls, and neighbourhood shops are either missing or unlabeled.
Section 3
Where Does POI Data Come From?
There are two fundamentally different types of POI databases in the world. The choice between them is the reason some maps look richer than others in India.
🏢
Type 1 — Proprietary Databases
Built and maintained by companies with dedicated teams. They hire ground truth collectors, partner with local businesses, and cross-verify using satellite imagery, Street View cars, and business registrations.

Who builds them: Google, Apple, Microsoft (Bing), HERE (owned by German automakers), TomTom.

India coverage: Excellent. Google has been collecting India data since 2008 — 16+ years of field collection, local business listings, and user contributions.

Cost to use: Expensive. Google Maps charges roughly ₹580 per 1,000 map loads. At 2,000 daily active users, that is ₹35+ lakhs per year in map costs alone.
🌍
Type 2 — OpenStreetMap (OSM)
A community-built, open-source world map — like Wikipedia but for geography. Anyone can add or edit data. No single company owns it.

Who builds it: Volunteers, NGOs, humanitarian organisations, and technology companies that contribute back. Over 10 million registered contributors worldwide.

India coverage: Variable. Major cities (Bangalore, Delhi, Mumbai) have good coverage from tech-community volunteers. Smaller cities and tier-2 towns can be sparse.

Cost to use: Free. The data is open licence. Tile rendering services like CartoDB, Stadia, and MapTiler build on top of OSM and offer it at low or no cost.
🔍
OSM in Bangalore — the honest picture
Bangalore is one of India's best-mapped cities on OSM because the tech community here actively contributes. Major roads, BBMP ward boundaries, metro stations, and large landmarks are very well covered. However, the smaller neighbourhood-level POIs — the kirana near the apartment complex, the tuition centre on the corner, the local clinic — are often missing or incomplete. This is why CartoDB Voyager (which uses OSM) shows fewer labels than Google Maps in the same area.

The uncomfortable truth: switching OSM providers does not solve the India POI gap
MapTiler and Stadia both use OpenStreetMap data — the same underlying database as CartoDB. Their maps look prettier (better fonts, better colours, better iconography) but they will show the same POIs in Bangalore, because the data source is identical. Switching from CartoDB to Stadia would be a style upgrade, not a data upgrade. The only way to get richer POI labels is to pay for a proprietary data provider (Google or HERE), which is not financially viable at MVP scale.
Section 5
MapIt's Approach — The Overpass API
Instead of trying to make the map tiles show more POIs, MapIt will bring the POI information directly into the listing card. This is what Session 5 builds.

✅
Why this approach is actually better than map tile labels
A label on a map tile is passive — the buyer has to zoom in, look around, and mentally measure distances. The Overpass approach puts the information directly in the listing card with exact distances. "450m from JSS School" is more useful to a parent evaluating a property than a school icon somewhere on a map that may or may not be the right school. The information comes to the buyer; the buyer does not have to hunt for it.
How the Overpass API works
  • 1
    Seller posts a listing with GPS coordinates
    The seller drops a pin on the map for their property or item. MapIt records the exact latitude and longitude (e.g. 12.9352, 77.6245).
  • 2
    MapIt queries the Overpass API in the background
    Immediately after the listing is saved, MapIt silently sends a query to the Overpass API (a free public service that reads OpenStreetMap data): "Find all schools, supermarkets, hospitals, and malls within 2km of these coordinates." This happens in the background — the seller does not wait for it.
  • 3
    Results are saved with the listing
    Overpass returns a list of nearby POIs with names and coordinates. MapIt calculates the walking distance to each and saves the top 3–5 results inside the listing's data. This is stored once and never needs to be fetched again.
  • 4
    Buyers see the "📍 Nearby" section in the listing card
    When a buyer opens the listing detail, they see a "Nearby" section showing the closest school, supermarket, hospital, and mall with exact distances. No extra clicks, no zooming the map, no guessing.
💰
Cost
The Overpass API is completely free. No API key, no account, no rate limit for moderate use. The data comes from OpenStreetMap — the same database used by CartoDB — but queried specifically for each listing's location.
📊
Data quality in India
OSM POI data quality in major Indian cities (Bangalore, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai) is good enough to show the prominent schools, hospitals, and supermarkets near most listings. It will not be perfect — some smaller POIs will be missing — but it covers the high-value use cases that buyers care about most.

Session 8 — Decision gate
Evaluate: Is Overpass good enough? Should we upgrade the tile provider?
After Session 5 UAT feedback on the Overpass POI card, decide: (a) Is the nearby POI data good enough for the 4-city launch? (b) Is the map tile itself causing buyer confusion or drop-off? If yes to (b), upgrade to MapTiler Streets (~$50/month) which offers better India rendering at a manageable cost. If Overpass covers the use case, stay on CartoDB Voyager and save the budget.
📌 Key Takeaway for the Strategy Discussion
  • POI labels on map tiles are drawn from the tile provider's database — you get what they have, you cannot add to it.
  • All free tile providers (CartoDB, Stadia, MapTiler) use the same OpenStreetMap database. Switching between them is a style upgrade, not a data upgrade — India POI coverage stays the same.
  • The only way to get richer POI labels on the map itself is to pay for Google Maps or HERE Maps — both are expensive at scale (₹5–35 lakhs per year at 4-city launch volumes).
  • MapIt's Session 5 Overpass approach puts the POI information directly inside the listing card with exact distances. This is arguably more useful to buyers than labels on a background map.
  • The tile provider question is deferred to Session 8 — after we can evaluate whether the Overpass card satisfies real buyer needs in UAT, before spending on a tile upgrade.
  • Budget context: Overpass API costs ₹0. Google Maps at 500 DAU costs ₹35L/year. MapTiler at 500 DAU costs ₹50–100/month (~₹60–120K/year). The decision is a value-vs-cost trade-off best made after Session 5 data.
